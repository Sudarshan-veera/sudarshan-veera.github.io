Notes

Taping out a SKY130 ASIC as a student

The TinyTapeout submission meant working within real silicon constraints rather than simulation-only limits — an 80% tile utilization ceiling, a strict power budget, and gate-level checks that don't forgive shortcuts. The most instructive part was debugging forbidden met5 PDN violations and cocotb X-value errors: each fix required tracing the issue back through the physical design flow in OpenROAD rather than just the RTL. It reframed hardware design as a negotiation with the fabrication process, not just the logic.

04.3

Engineering Logbook

Short post-mortems from my own projects: what broke, why, and what fixed it.

LOG 03TinyTapeout · SKY130

Two bugs, one submission: met5 straps and X-values

Problem 1: met5. The hardening flow kept putting power-grid straps on met5, and TinyTapeout reserves that layer for its own top-level grid, so the check failed. The default flow builds a multi-layer power distribution network (PDN), and that is where met5 came from.

Fix: I set PDN_MULTILAYER: false in src/user_config.json so the grid stays on lower layers. Lesson: once I edited the config by hand, I never re-ran --create-user-config, because it would overwrite my overrides. A few tall met5 straps were still being investigated afterwards, so I check the report after every run.

Problem 2: X-values in cocotb. The gate-level simulation showed X (unknown) on the outputs. My moving-average detector keeps a window of past samples in flip-flops, and in the gate-level netlist those start unknown. Until the window is full, every average is X, and the X spreads to the anomaly flag.

Fix: pre-fill the window with known samples first, then poll the READY signal instead of waiting a fixed number of clock cycles. The test now waits for the design instead of guessing.

Takeaway: reset and warm-up matter more in gate-level sim than RTL. RTL forgives; the netlist does not.

LOG 02ANGEL BOT · ESP32-CAM

Power notes for rev 2 (planning, not measured yet)

Rescue robots fail when the power rail dips, and the symptom is always confusing: random resets that look like software bugs. These are my design notes for the next revision, not measured results.

Where the current goes. Streaming video over Wi-Fi makes the ESP32-CAM draw short, large current spikes, typically a few hundred mA, so check the module datasheet. The MQ-135 gas sensor has a heater element that draws a steady current of its own, and motors add noisy surges. The INMP441 microphone barely registers.

Plan. (1) Give the motors their own driver and supply path, sharing only ground with the logic. (2) Put a bulk capacitor right at the camera module's supply pins to absorb Wi-Fi bursts. (3) Use a regulator rated well above the worst-case peak, not the average. (4) Let the sensors warm up before trusting their readings, and wake them only when needed.

Security note. While reviewing the firmware I also flagged hard-coded credentials. They belong in a separate config that is never committed, and the old tokens should be rotated.

Takeaway: design for the peak, not the average, and measure it with a scope before trusting it.

LOG 01THz metamaterial sensor · CST

How the sensor design evolved: D1 to D4

D1. A plain square ring resonator, which gave resonances near 3.8 THz and 4.5 THz. Two peaks, so no clean sensing line yet.

D2. I cut discontinuities into the conductive path. That changes the effective inductance and capacitance, which moves and reshapes the peaks.

D3. I connected diagonal elements to the outer loop, which nudged the resonance slightly.

D4. Two diagonal paths tie into the outer loop. The current path gets longer, so the 4.5 THz resonance moves down to 3.892 THz, and the absorption there improves. The final quality factor is about 30.22.

Tuning the material. Raising the GaAs conductivity raises the absorption peak, and it gets close to 1 near 7×105 S/m, while the band narrows a little and shifts up slightly.

Using it. A soil sample changes the surrounding permittivity, which shifts the peak. With Topp's equation, soil moisture of 1 % to 40 % maps to a permittivity of about 2.3 up to 24.96.

Takeaway: every geometry change is an L or C change. Think in current paths, then confirm in the simulator.

How the THz sensor works drag the moisture slider

  1. Resonator. The patterned GaAs layer on aluminium and a 50 µm polyimide base absorbs a THz wave strongly at one frequency, 3.892 THz, with Q = 30.22.
  2. Soil changes the electric field. Wet soil has a much higher permittivity than dry soil (water is about 80, dry minerals 3 to 5). Topp's equation links moisture to permittivity, and the paper's table runs from 2.3 at 1 % VWC to 24.96 at 40 %.
  3. The peak moves. The extra permittivity adds capacitance, so the resonance shifts. Reading the new peak frequency tells you the moisture, with no soil sampling.

The Topp curve is matched to the paper's table end points. The size of the shift (sensitivity) is an illustrative slider, not exported CST data.

How the tunable dual-band FSS works varactor + graphene

  1. Absorber. A resonant layer sits above a ground plane. Energy that is neither reflected nor stored is burnt in the lossy parts (the 5000 Ω/sq sheet), so reflection drops to almost zero at the resonances.
  2. Varactors set the frequency. Raising the varactor capacitance from 0.2 to 1.1 pF slides band 1 from 2.785 to 1.679 GHz and band 2 from 6.180 to 5.011 GHz. The two bands change width in opposite directions.
  3. Graphene sets the depth. The graphene capacitor changes the layer's resistance. Band 1 is matched best near 0.17 eV and band 2 near 0.07 eV, so one band can be pushed deep while the other is held back.

Band positions and widths use the end points from the report (Table 4.1) and depths follow its Tables 4.2 and Section 4.3. Values in between are interpolated with a simple dip model, so this is a teaching model, not a CST export.
